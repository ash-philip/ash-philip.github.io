AP-001 · TaskFlow Growth Analytics

Why a 9x lift in upgrades was an illusion.

A growth analytics case study on a simulated SaaS product, testing whether standard methods can tell a real effect from a misleading one.

By Ashwin Philip2 min readLive app

TaskFlow is a fictional project-management software company. I generated two years of customer data for it, covering 2,026 accounts from signup to cancellation, and analyzed it the way a growth team would: funnel, retention, experiments and customer lifetime. Because I built the data, I know the true answer behind every number. That lets the project test something most analyses can't: whether standard methods actually find the truth.

01 · problem

Which growth levers actually work?

Only a third of TaskFlow's signups ever became active users, and most free accounts never upgraded. I tested three changes, each aimed at a different stage of the funnel: a shorter onboarding flow to increase activation, a reframed discount to steer trial users toward annual plans, and an in-app nudge to move heavy free users to a paid plan. The goal was to find out which of these changes actually worked, and which only looked like they did.

02 · approach

A dataset with known answers.

Because the data is simulated, each experiment has a true effect built in, so the analysis can be checked against the real answer. I modeled the data in dbt on BigQuery, ran a power analysis to check whether each test had enough users to detect its effect, and, for the nudge, compared nudged users only with users who were equally active but never saw it.

03 · impact

One misleading win, two open questions.

Once users were compared fairly, the upgrade nudge showed no real effect: a difference of −0.4 percentage points, confirmed with three separate methods. The onboarding and discount tests were too small to give a reliable answer, so the conclusion is: not enough data yet.

The clearest opportunity sits earlier in the funnel. Only a third of signups become active users, but 79% of those who do go on to pay.

AP-002 · Transit & Rideshare Analysis

Higher fares alone may not be the way to go.

A forecasting and scenario-planning case study for a transit rideshare program, testing how fare changes and rising costs shape its financial outlook.

By Ashwin Philip2 min readLive app
How much of the program's cost do fares cover?
Share of operating costs covered by fares over the next 12 months, by scenario
Dashed line marks the base case. Ridership response to price assumed at −0.2 for a 5% increase and −0.3 for a 10% increase. Synthetic data.
readout

This project models the rideshare and vanpool program of a public transit agency. It is based on consulting work I did for the agency, rebuilt with fully synthetic data so it can be shared publicly. The data mirrors a real program's structure: a sharp drop in ridership in 2020, a gradual recovery, seasonal swings, and fuel, maintenance and insurance costs that move independently of each other.

01 · problem

Is the program still sustainable?

The program had not raised its fares in over a decade, while fuel prices and operating costs kept climbing. I set out to answer two questions: is the program still financially feasible, and how could fares be raised to improve cost recovery (the share of operating costs covered by fare revenue) and keep pace with the market?

02 · approach

Forecast first, then test the options.

I generated seven years of monthly operating data and built a validated pipeline that combines ridership, revenue and costs into a single KPI dataset. I then forecast the next 12 months of ridership with a seasonal time-series model (SARIMAX), and validated it by training on data through 2025 and testing on 2026: it beat a simple seasonal baseline by 22% on average error. Against that forecast, I tested four planning scenarios:

03 · impact

Fares help, but costs decide the outcome.

Raising fares does improve cost recovery. At current fares, fare revenue covers 87% of operating costs. A 5% increase raises that to 91%, and a 10% increase to 93%, though the larger increase loses about 3% of riders.

The risk is cost growth. If operating costs rise 8% alongside a 5% fare increase, cost recovery drops to 84%, lower than if fares had not changed at all.

recommendation

Raise fares moderately, around 5%, and pair the increase with active cost management. A fare increase on its own cannot keep pace with rising costs.

AP-003 · Brasimart E-Commerce Analytics

Late boxes cost 2.7 stars.

A customer and operations analytics case study on 96,000 real marketplace orders, looking at how delivery performance shapes the way customers rate their experience.

By Ashwin Philip2 min read
Does on-time delivery matter to customers?

readout

Brasimart is my analysis of the Olist dataset, a public record of real orders from a Brazilian online marketplace between 2016 and 2018. I designed a relational database for it in Azure SQL, loaded the data, and used SQL and Python to study how revenue grew, what customers are worth, and how delivery performance affects the way customers rate their orders.

01 · problem

What keeps marketplace customers happy?

A marketplace depends on repeat business, and repeat business depends on good experiences. I wanted to understand which parts of the operation shape the reviews customers leave, since reviews are what new buyers see first.

02 · approach

From raw tables to one view of every order.

I built the pipeline in three steps.

02Combine

03Analyze

Studied revenue over time, customer value and segments, and how delivery timing affects reviews.

03 · impact

Delivery reliability shapes satisfaction.

Most orders arrive well ahead of the promised date, about 12 days early on average, and those customers rate their orders highly, between 4.2 and 4.3 stars. Once an order is late, the average review falls to as low as 1.6 stars.

recommendation

Shorten padded delivery estimates, and protect the ones that are at risk.

  1. Tighten estimates on reliable routes. Three in four orders arrive at least a week early, and arriving extra early earns no better reviews. Shorter, accurate estimates make listings more competitive at checkout at no cost to satisfaction.
  2. Flag at-risk orders before they slip. Late orders average 1.6 stars. Identifying them early and notifying customers proactively protects the review.
  3. Track two numbers together. Measure late-delivery rate alongside average review score, so shorter estimates never come at the cost of more late orders.

AP-004 · Seattle Rental Neighborhood Wizard

Three questions. Find your neighborhood.

A self-serve BI dashboard in Tableau that helps renters new to Seattle find neighborhoods that fit their budget, commute and lifestyle, built entirely from public data.

By Ashwin Philip2 min readLive dashboard
How the wizard works
Click through the steps a renter sees
🔒 public.tableau.com/views/NeighborhoodFinderWizardOpen ↗

Moving to a new city means choosing a neighborhood before you know any of them. This project turns that choice into a three-step Tableau wizard. It scores all 53 of Seattle's neighborhoods against what a renter tells it, shows the matches on a map, and explains the top three in plain language. At its core it is a business intelligence project: turning scattered public data into one clean model, and designing a dashboard a non-technical user can understand and trust.

01 · problem

Rental sites don't tell you where to live.

Listing sites filter by price and bedrooms, but they can't tell a newcomer whether a neighborhood fits how they actually live: how long the commute is, whether they can walk to things, how close transit is, or what the area feels like on a weekend. I wanted one tool that weighs all of that together and gives a clear answer.

02 · approach

Six signals, one match score.

I built the data layer in Python, cleaning Census, OpenStreetMap and transit data and aligning all of it to Seattle's 53 neighborhood boundaries. Every neighborhood was then scored on six dimensions.

The dimensions combine into a single weighted match score from 0 to 100. In Tableau, I used parameters and parameter actions to build the step-by-step flow, so each answer narrows the map before the next question refines it.

03 · impact

Self-serve BI for non-technical users.

Instead of a table of numbers, a renter gets three named neighborhoods, a score for each, the strongest reason it fits, and the tradeoff to expect. In the example above, a renter with a $2,300 budget for a one-bedroom was matched with Georgetown, the University District and Miller Park.

The same principle applies to any business dashboard: guide the user to a decision, rather than handing them a table and expecting them to find it.

key design choice

Always give an answer. If a budget is tight, a near-miss tier shows the closest matches instead of an empty map, so every user leaves with options.
